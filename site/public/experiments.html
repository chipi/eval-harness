02Ties

The top is a crowd, not a podium.

A leaderboard sorts, so something is always first. The question is whether first is distinguishable from second. Here every leader was tested against every other arm on the same items, with a paired permutation test and a correction for running many comparisons at once.

In 5 of 6 experiments the leader cannot be told apart from a group of others — on summarisation, from 15 of them. The cheapest paid model inside that group cost a fraction of the dearest: 143× less on summarisation.

The one clean winner is a 499 MB tagger fine-tuned on Few-NERD's own training data, separated from all 27 rivals.

03Fine-tuning

Train a small model on your data. Yours.

  1. A free local model was first, or not separable from first, in 5 of 6.

    Against hosted LLMs run with one prompt, no examples and no reasoning. Every one is under 2 GB and needs no GPU — $0 per item — against hosted models billing up to $2.01 for 100 items (anthropic_l, SummEval).

  2. The clearest wins were on text like the text it was trained on.

    The same BART architecture, fine-tuned on XSum's one-sentence BBC summaries instead of CNN/DailyMail's, finishes last of 29 — below copying the first three sentences.

  3. On one task, even a free model trained for it lost to LLM judges — and the dearest judge was not needed.

    Judging summaries, scored against the mean of two experts as one expert is against the other two, the best judges reach 93% of a human's agreement (the best of 24, chosen on the same articles; 92% held out); UniEval, the best free scorer and an evaluator trained for the task, reaches 64%. The judges rank all 16 summaries in one call while UniEval scores one at a time, and a pointwise LLM judge was not run; a no-model lookup of which system wrote each summary reaches 63%. The cheapest judge the leader cannot be separated from, gemma_l, has open weights and bills $278 per million articles — 1/23 of the leader. One task and one trained evaluator, and why it lost is not tested: the direction, not the size.

  4. And part of a fine-tune's lead is learning the dataset's own conventions.

    On AG News, the lead of the three fine-tunes is concentrated in 17 items the hosted field mostly gets wrong. Adjudicated by topic by one reader shown the hosted answer, the gold was wrong on all 17, and on that gold every hosted LLM scores above every fine-tune. On Few-NERD, 57% of the winner's margin over openai_m is one entity type the prompt never defined (30–57% across the hosted arms). The leaders are better at the benchmark; how much of that is better at the task is a separate question, measured in each report.

04Trust

Can you trust any of this?

Five experiments score a sample of 200–280 items, not a full test set, and SummEval scores the whole of its 100. So each one also runs a calibration: one well-known model or metric, through this repo's own code, on the benchmark's full test set (or a large seeded sample), compared with the number its authors published.

Calibration found one real error — in our sample, not our scorer.

The summarisation experiment took the first 200 articles of the CNN/DailyMail test set. The test set lists CNN's articles first, so all 200 were CNN's, while the benchmark is 91% DailyMail. Lead-3 scored ROUGE-1 30.20 on that slice against 39.94 on the full test set; CNN articles are simply harder to summarise from their lead.

Re-run on 200 DailyMail articles drawn at random, the five local summarisers kept every verdict they had on CNN: the fine-tune still beats lead-3 by the same margin, and the XSum model is still last. The hosted models were not re-run, so the summarisation price findings are scoped to CNN's articles — and say so.

No figure on this site is typed in by hand. Every figure is recomputed from the committed runs each time the site is built, and every number the prose quotes must appear in the report it comes from, or the build fails. Behind the reports, a checker verifies 188 claims against the runs on every commit.

06Reproduce it

Built to be checked — by you.

The harness, every run, every model output and every analysis script are in the repository. Five levels of checking, each optional, each a few commands.

  1. 00

    Verify

    Every number in every report, recomputed from the committed predictions.

    $0 · one command
  2. 01

    Data

    Fetch the public benchmarks and hash-check every item against what was measured.

    $0
  3. 02

    Local

    Rerun the 35 free arms — local models and baselines — on your CPU, pinned to the same weights.

    $0 · CPU hours
  4. 03

    Hosted

    Rerun the 24 LLMs with one OpenRouter key. The test: does every separation still hold?

    $19.07 billed, all six
  5. 04

    Extend

    Add a model of your own and test it against the whole committed field.

    one config
# every number, from the committed runs
$ make verify
# rerun an experiment and check its findings
$ make plan      EXP=scifact
$ make data      EXP=scifact
$ make reproduce EXP=scifact LEVEL=local
$ make compare   EXP=scifact
