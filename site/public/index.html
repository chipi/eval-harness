02Ties

The top is a crowd, not a podium.

A leaderboard sorts, so something is always first. The question is whether first is distinguishable from second. Here every leader was tested against every other arm on the same items, with a paired permutation test and a correction for running many comparisons at once.

In 4 of 5 experiments the leader cannot be told apart from a group of others — on summarisation, from 15 of them. The cheapest paid model inside that group cost a fraction of the dearest: 143× less on summarisation.

The one clean winner is a 499 MB tagger fine-tuned on Few-NERD's own training data, separated from all 27 rivals.

03Fine-tuning

Train a small model on your data. Yours.

  1. The best fine-tuned model was first, or tied for first, in 5 of 5.

    Every one is under 2 GB and needs no GPU — $0 per item — against hosted models billing up to $1.97 for 200 items (anthropic_l, CNN/DailyMail).

  2. But only on text like the text it was trained on.

    The same BART architecture, fine-tuned on XSum's one-sentence BBC summaries instead of CNN/DailyMail's, finishes last of 29 — below copying the first three sentences.

  3. And part of a fine-tune's lead is learning the dataset's own conventions.

    On AG News, nearly the whole lead of the three fine-tunes sits in 17 items whose gold labels most of the hosted field disputes. On Few-NERD, 57% of the winner's margin is one entity type whose meaning exists only in that corpus. The leaders are better at the benchmark; how much of that is better at the task is a separate question, measured in each report.

04Trust

Can you trust any of this?

Every experiment scores a sample of 200–280 items, not a full test set. So each one also runs a calibration: one well-known model, through this repo's own code, on the benchmark's full test set (or a large seeded sample), compared with the number its authors published.

Calibration found one real error — in our sample, not our scorer.

The summarisation experiment took the first 200 articles of the CNN/DailyMail test set. The test set lists CNN's articles first, so all 200 were CNN's, while the benchmark is 91% DailyMail. Lead-3 scored ROUGE-1 30.20 on that slice against 39.94 on the full test set; CNN articles are simply harder to summarise from their lead.

Re-run on 200 DailyMail articles drawn at random, the five local summarisers kept every verdict they had on CNN: the fine-tune still beats lead-3 by the same margin, and the XSum model is still last. The hosted models were not re-run, so the summarisation price findings are scoped to CNN's articles — and say so.

Nothing on this site is typed in by hand. Every figure is recomputed from the committed runs each time the site is built, and every number the prose quotes must appear in the report it comes from, or the build fails. 06The method

These answers took a method, not a model.

Nothing here needed special access or a big budget. It took six habits — each learned by getting something wrong first — and a harness that enforces them. They work on any problem you can put into a dataset. The framing builds on Chip Huyen's AI Engineering and Designing Machine Learning Systems.

01An eval is an experiment.Vary one thing. Record everything else.02The data is the instrument.Freeze it, hash it, check it against the world.03Silver is not gold.A model's answers rank; a person's answers judge.04Measure the noise before the difference.A delta below an arm's own jitter is nothing.05Tested, not read.Say separated, not higher.06Decide on the record.The scorer is part of the experiment. So is the reason.

Built to be checked — by you.
