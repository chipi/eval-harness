The method · six habits · learned the hard way

How to know what you think you know.

The findings on this site took no special model and no big budget. They took a method — six habits, each learned by getting something wrong first. Here they are as the experiments taught them, with the step in the harness that keeps each one, and what it means for your own work.

01An eval is an experiment.02The data is the instrument.03Silver is not gold.04Measure the noise before the difference.05Tested, not read.06Decide on the record.

The lifecycle every experiment here followed. Each habit lights the step it protects.

01Experiment

An eval is an experiment.

The same 24 hosted models (the cheapest twelve on SciFact) ran on six tasks with one prompt per task, at temperature 0 with reasoning off (on SummEval, Qwen's large slot is a stand-in, qwen37max) — so when a model's score differs between two tasks, the task is what changed, apart from what each report states: on SummEval a stand-in model, prices a month later and a different calling machine.

Every run carries a fingerprint: hashes of the data, the prompt and the scoring code, the model behind the alias, library versions, the commit. Before the harness shows a single delta between two runs, it lists the fields that moved.

The habitChange one thing per arm, and record everything else — so “we only changed the prompt” is checkable rather than claimed.
In the harness
$ make run-compare BASE=… CAND=…
Fingerprints DIFFER. Fields that moved: arm.params.sentences

In your project Your arms are your candidates — prompts, models, retrieval settings, thresholds — one YAML file each.

02Data

The data is the instrument.

The summarisation sample was the first 200 articles of the CNN/DailyMail test set. The test set lists CNN's articles first, so every one was CNN's, while the benchmark is mostly DailyMail. Copying the first three sentences scored ROUGE-1 30.20 on that slice against 39.94 on the full test set.

No check in the harness could see it: every one compared the repository with itself. One calibration row — a known baseline against its published value — caught it. Now every experiment has one, and 11 of 11 agree with the literature, 10 of them inside their own interval.

The habitFreeze the data and hash it, so a number always means the same items. Then check your sample against the population it claims to represent.
In the harness
$ make dataset-create · make dataset-materialize
a changed input fails loudly instead of silently measuring different bytes

In your project Your dataset is a set of real inputs, frozen. Sample it on purpose — by date, by customer, by difficulty — and write down what it covers.

03Ground truth

Silver is not gold.

In the tutorial, the system that wrote the silver references scores a perfect 1.000 against them and ranks first. Re-scored against six summaries a person wrote, it falls to second — and no arm can be told apart from any other (p = 0.2124).

On AG News, the lead of the three fine-tunes is concentrated in 17 items the hosted field mostly gets wrong. Adjudicated by topic by one reader shown the hosted answer, the gold was wrong on all 17, and on that gold every hosted LLM scores above every fine-tune. The dataset's own gold stays primary; the adjudicated one sits beside it. Disputed gold is a finding, not noise.

An AI judge is silver too, and SummEval measures how good: scored against the mean of two experts, as one expert is against the other two, the best judges reach 93% of a human's agreement (the best of 24, chosen on the same articles; 92% held out). The experts are not noiseless either: 41 of 44 groups of word-for-word identical summaries got different expert scores. Close to a person is not the same as a person.

Two of those judges re-measured the summarisation experiment and ranked every hosted LLM above every BART model. The one blind human check on those outputs, one non-expert reader on 20 pairs, preferred bart_l 14 to 6. That check is weak too (p = 0.115, truncated articles), so neither is confirmed on those outputs; on crowd ratings of 2022 LLM summaries the judges track people better than one rater does on coherence and relevance, not on faithfulness, and not system by system. So the harness refuses a judge until it is validated, and the summarisation experiment still ranks by coverage.

The habitSilver gets you started; gold tells you the truth. Write a small gold set early, check silver — and any AI judge — against it, and read every item where strong systems agree against the gold.
In the harness
$ make rescore REFERENCE=gold/… · make silver-calibrate · make judge-score
re-score old runs against new gold for $0, measure how far silver drifts, re-measure with judges you have validated

In your project A small set of answers written by someone who knows the domain is worth more than a large set a model generated: it is the only one that can tell you the model is wrong.

04Noise

Measure the noise before the difference.

BART's apparent lead at n=20 was 0.0737; across 200 articles it was 0.00008. Two independent 100-article evals of the same models agree at only ρ = 0.79.

A pilot correlates beautifully and picks the wrong winner — in three of the five experiments here that ran one.

The habitRun every arm more than once and measure how far it disagrees with itself. Tune on a small dev slice, report on the rest, and measure how many items a ranking needs instead of assuming it.
In the harness
$ make experiment-run REPEAT=3 · make holdout · make rank-stability
treat deltas below 0.044479 as noise

In your project Before you believe the new prompt is a few points better, run the old one twice.

05Significance

Tested, not read.

A leaderboard sorts, so something is always first. In 5 of 6 experiments here the leader cannot be separated from a group of others — on summarisation, from 15 of them, the cheapest paid one 143× cheaper than the dearest model in the field.

Every separation claim on this site rests on a paired permutation test per item, with a Holm correction for the number of comparisons made at once. A tie means the test could not separate two arms at this many items, not that they are equal. Intervals elsewhere (the human ceiling) are bootstrapped, and the price slopes are fits, not tests.

The habitDecide the comparisons before reading the table, correct for how many you made, and report a tie as a tie.
In the harness
$ make family-test · make leaderboard
separated from 6 of 18 opponents   ← SciFact's leader, glm_s

In your project When the answer is “tied”, look at the gap and the sample size first: if the gap is one you can live with, the cheapest member of the tie is the decision.

06The record

Decide on the record.

Running the experiments found eight defects in the harness's own instrument, each of which changed published numbers or would have. Three of these — the parser, the unparsed-as-empty rule, the reasoning flag — moved a single arm by more than the gap separating most of the field. The scoring code is a model-sized lever.

Every correction is kept: the lab notebook records the findings that were retracted along the way, several caused by this harness's own scorer.

The habitHash and test the scorer, re-score stored outputs when it changes, and promote a baseline only with a reason that says what the tests showed.
In the harness
$ make rescore · make run-promote REASON="…" · make validate
re-scoring is $0; validation exits non-zero, so it belongs in CI

In your project Keep a journal. The mistakes you write down are the checks you add next.

07Standing on

Built on Chip Huyen's framing.

The approach in this repository builds on Chip Huyen's books. Their framing — evaluation is engineering work on your data, a model is chosen on a private leaderboard rather than a public one, the dataset is a design decision, every experiment is tracked and versioned — is the foundation this harness was built on. What this project adds is a tool that enforces it, and six experiments that show what skipping it costs.

The idea-by-idea mapping, with chapters, is in Influences.

  • Chip Huyen — AI Engineering (O'Reilly, 2025). Ch. 2: sampling makes models probabilistic, and so inconsistent — measured here at temperature 0. Ch. 3–4: why public benchmarks rarely pick the model for your application, private leaderboards, AI judges as subjective evaluation to be checked against exact and human evaluation. Ch. 8: dataset engineering — quality, coverage, quantity.
  • Chip Huyen — Designing Machine Learning Systems (O'Reilly, 2022). Ch. 4: sampling and labelling. Ch. 6: baselines, experiment tracking and versioning. Ch. 8: distribution shift.
  • Martin Zinkevich — Rules of Machine Learning (Google). Design and implement your metrics first.
  • Breck et al. — The ML Test Score (2017). Tests for data, models and infrastructure.
  • Bernardi et al. — 150 Successful Machine Learning Models (KDD 2019, Booking.com). Offline gains do not automatically become real ones.
  • More, curated by Chip Huyen: huyenchip.com/mlops
